Federal Crimes, Explained

Every crime in Title 18 (Crimes) of the United States Code, the main body of federal criminal law. Each shows the full official text, color-coded to show the prohibited act, intent, and penalty, followed by a plain-English summary.

How to read this page
  • Open any card for the official text of the law, followed by a plain-English summary, key points, and the penalty.
  • In the official text, the prohibited act (what a person does that makes it a crime) is highlighted in orange, words about intent (such as knowingly) in purple, and penalties in red. Intent often decides whether something is a crime at all.
  • Dotted-underlined words are legal terms. Tap or hover to see what they mean.
  • References to other laws are links. Links to sections on this page jump there; others open the official text on GovInfo.
  • The colored bar on each card shows the maximum prison time. Most people convicted receive less. Actual sentences depend on the facts and the federal sentencing guidelines.
  • A felony is a crime punishable by more than one year in prison; a misdemeanor is one year or less.

    Section headings and statutory text are from the official GovInfo edition of the U.S. Code (U.S. Government Publishing Office). Plain-English summaries, key points, penalty summaries, crime types, maximum-penalty labels, and the highlighting of prohibited acts were prepared with AI assistance from that text. They are simplified and may contain errors, so rely on the official text.

    Not included: repealed sections and sections that do not themselves create a crime (definitions, procedure, civil remedies, forfeiture rules, and sections that only add penalties to crimes defined elsewhere). Statutory notes are omitted. Many federal crimes are also defined outside Title 18, for example drug crimes (Title 21) and tax crimes (Title 26); those are not covered here.

    This is general information, not legal advice. If you are facing a legal issue, talk to a lawyer. If you can't afford one and are charged with a federal crime, you have the right to a court-appointed attorney.
